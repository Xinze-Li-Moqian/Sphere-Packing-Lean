This is the section where a geometric problem becomes an analytic one, and after it the word "packing" nearly stops appearing.

## The bound

Suppose you can find a function $f$ on $\mathbb{R}^d$ with three properties:

- $f(x) \le 0$ once $\|x\| \ge 1$,
- $\hat f \ge 0$ everywhere,
- $\hat f(0) > 0$.

Then **every** packing of balls of radius $1/2$ has density at most $$\frac{f(0)}{\hat f(0)} \cdot \operatorname{vol}\big(B_d(0,\tfrac12)\big).$$

No packing is named. One function, chosen once, bounds all of them.

## Why it works

Sum $f$ over the lattice of a periodic packing and apply Poisson summation.

On the left, the centres are at least $1$ apart, so every term except the one at the origin sits where $f \le 0$. The sum is therefore at most $f(0)$.

On the right, every term is $\ge 0$ because $\hat f$ is, so the sum is at least the single term $\hat f(0)$, divided by the covolume.

Putting the two together, $f(0) \ge \hat f(0)/\operatorname{covol}$, which says the lattice cannot be too tight. Divide by the volume of a ball and that is the bound.

Both hypotheses are used exactly once, and each is used to throw away everything except one term. That is the whole proof, and it is why the conditions look the way they do: they are precisely what is needed to make each side collapse to its origin term.

## The catch

The bound is only as good as the function. Cohn and Elkies computed numerically that in dimension $8$ there ought to exist an $f$ making it equal to the $E_8$ density exactly — and could not produce one. That function stayed missing for thirteen years.

Asking for equality is a severe demand. Equality forces both inequalities above to be equalities, which forces $f$ to vanish at every distance $E_8$ uses — $\sqrt{2n}$ for all $n \ge 1$ — and $\hat f$ to vanish there too, since $E_8$ is its own dual. And because $f \le 0$ beyond radius $1$ while touching $0$ at those points, each zero must be **double**: the graph comes up to the axis and turns back without crossing.

So the remaining task is a construction problem with an exact specification: a Schwartz function with prescribed double zeros, whose Fourier transform has the same double zeros and a sign. The last two sections build it.
